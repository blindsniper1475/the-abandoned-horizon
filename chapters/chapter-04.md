## Chapter Text

David had been awake since six, and by nine he had admitted, privately, in the log he kept in his head because there was no device left worth trusting, that he was losing.

Not dramatically. That was the part nobody else seemed to see yet. There was no monster in the room, no wall bleeding, no clock running backward. There was just the arithmetic, going round and round, coming back the same every time, and the same being wrong.

He sat at the long table in the common room with the folders stacked to his left and a legal pad to his right, and the legal pad had four columns on it: SYSTEM, OBSERVED, EXPECTED, DELTA. Forty-one rows. He'd started it the night of their arrival, when the building's hum had stopped all at once instead of winding down, and he'd added to it every hour since, in the way other men his age added to a golf handicap or a blood-pressure chart. Deliberately. As if the number itself were a kind of medicine.

The heating system held sixty-eight degrees everywhere, floor to floor, including rooms with the doors shut and rooms with the windows single-pane and forty years old. Delta: zero. Impossible, but zero. The electrical load — he'd found the panel off the boiler room and stood there for twenty minutes reading breakers like scripture — ran steady at a draw that meant something was using power somewhere in the building, constantly, on every circuit, in amounts too small to name and too consistent to be leakage. Delta: unknown. The water pressure cycled. Not up and down like a well pump hunting. Cycled, the way a program cycles, on an interval he'd timed three times now and gotten 47 minutes, 47 minutes, and 46 and a half. Delta: minus thirty seconds, unexplained.

Forty-one rows. Half of them read *zero*, which should have reassured him, because zero meant working, and working meant explicable, and explicable meant solvable. The other half read *unknown*, and *unknown* was not a number, and he could not patch a delta he couldn't measure, and here was the thing he kept circling without landing on: a building abandoned fifteen years did not hold sixty-eight degrees. A building nobody paid bills for did not run a constant load. A building with no staff did not keep its water on a timer. Every row on the pad, taken alone, had an explanation a reasonable man would accept — maintenance crew, insulation, municipal supply, timers left set by the last superintendent and never switched off. Taken together, the explanations canceled each other out, because each one required a person, and there were no people, and the five of them counted fifty dormitory doors and slept in two made-up rooms apiece while two more stayed lit for nobody, and the thermos in their room was full every morning, and nobody had ever found the kitchen that poured it.

His phone said 9:14. His phone said no signal for eleven miles. His phone also, since yesterday, said 9:14 sometimes at what felt like noon, and he had stopped trusting it, which cost him more than he wanted to say out loud, because his whole method was timestamps, and a timestamp you couldn't trust was like a ruler that changed length between uses. So he'd gone analog. He carried the school's own wall clock in his head against the storm outside — light, dark, light, dark — and marked rows by meals instead of hours, and the method made him feel about twelve years old, and it was the best he had.

"You're doing the thing," Sarah said.

She came down the gallery stairs with her coat belt loose and her hair done up in the efficient way she'd learned young, that kept it out of her face without vanity, and she stopped behind him and looked at the pad the way she looked at everything: registering it completely, committing nothing to her face.

"What thing."

"The narrating." She pulled out the chair beside him and sat, angling the seat so she could see both him and the walkway windows. She always did that. David had noticed it months ago and filed it under Sarah-thing-that-is-fine. Now it looked like training. "I can hear you running probabilities. You get a little fast, and you say 'in the likely case,' and then you tell me the odds that the coffee comes from a person versus the odds that it doesn't, and I don't want the odds that it doesn't."

"I wasn't—" He heard himself, stopped. "Sorry. Habit."

"It was funny for the first ten years." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "It's not a joke anymore. Don't do the dead-air one, either. I can feel you deciding not to do it."

Marcus came in from the direction of the main doors at 9:40, or what he and David had agreed to call 9:40, boots dry, which they'd also started writing down — dry, damp, wet, snow-melt — because it was the only outdoor reading they could take and it hadn't varied once. He'd been checking the vestibule again. He did that now the way other men checked a lock twice. Elena followed him with her notebook open to the map page, and she didn't sit, which told David the conversation was going to be standing-up and short.

"We're calling it the east stair," Elena said, before anyone asked, "because I'm not drawing another one. What I drew yesterday was wrong in ways I can point to, and I can't fix what I can't see, so we use words. East stair, service stairs, library, gallery, vaults."

"Vaults plural?" David said.

"One room. They call it vaults on the plates." She set the notebook down and turned it around so the four of them could see the copied ledger lines — her handwriting getting smaller as the entries got stranger, a habit of hers he recognized and envied, because shrinking your letters was at least a way of admitting something. "Your pad has forty-one rows. Mine has four lines. I think yours is going to kill you slower."

"Elena—"

"Fine. Standing-up and short." But she touched Marcus's sleeve once, lightly, on her way past, and whatever she'd brought down from the gallery died there, which was the other thing about this pair that used to be funny.

Jake was last down, camera bag across his chest even indoors, and he announced he'd slept great, thanks, better than in any bed he owned, and if anyone wanted to make that weird he was prepared to leave the room. Nobody let him make it weird. Coffee got poured from the thermos — full, unscrewed, exactly to the line — and David watched Sarah pour herself a cup without flinching and thought *she decided not to ask about it,* and wrote nothing down, because some rows weren't his.

They went over the folders again at the table, spread out like a census. Nobody had said what the numbers might mean yet, out loud, in a sentence, and David had figured it was because saying it made it a claim, and a claim needed a model, and his models kept dying. But everyone had picked one, he was sure, the way everyone picks a diagnosis before the doctor confirms it. Jonas Fells, eighty-eight, who'd answered the letter and hadn't shown at the airport. The percentages sat at the top-right of each folder like grades, and Alex Voss's red hundred sat at the end of the drawer like a graduation requirement nobody mentioned.

"That's the pattern," David said, before he decided whether it was a pattern, hearing himself go fast. "In the likely case, the numbers correlate with recency of file activity — look, Voss's stack is dated yesterday, the rest of ours are current within days, Fells's folder hasn't been touched since—there's no date on the last entry, which means either nobody updated it or it got updated and the update isn't logged, and those two options aren't the same size of problem—"

"David."

"—and the missing date is actually the loudest thing in the drawer, it's just that the red ink is louder to look at, so nobody's asking why Fells has no last-entry stamp when every single other folder in that cabinet does, including the ones with no visible difference—"

Sarah put her hand flat on the pad. Not on his arm. On the pad, covering the DELTA column, which was how she knew he'd stop faster than if she'd covered his mouth.

"In the likely case," she said, quiet, "you're about to tell us the odds that Jonas Fells is dead versus the odds he isn't. And I'm telling you, as someone who has to look at a photograph of him every time she walks past the trophy case, that I don't want those odds tonight."

He shut up. That was the thing about Sarah — she never raised her voice, she just gave you the exact cost of continuing.

Elena uncapped her pen, capped it, uncapped it again. "There's a fifth line in the ledger," she said. "Yesterday I copied four. There's a fifth, lower down, in the same upright hand, dated the same day as the others. It says *transfer complete* and then a word I can't read, and then a number."

"A number like a percentage?" David said, grateful for a real question.

"A number like a time. Twenty-three fifty-nine."

Boiler-room silence, and then Jake, easy as anything: "That's midnight, babe. Somebody wrote down midnight twice in a book that isn't supposed to be written in." And they all laughed — short, relieved, the laugh you get when a joke converts a haunting into paperwork — and David wrote TRANSFER COMPLETE / 23:59 on a fresh row and marked the system column *ledger* and the expected column blank, because he refused to write down what he expected, and the refusal cost him something he could feel happening, like a tooth going numb.

That was when the fans stopped.

Not the gradual sag of a blower losing speed — the flat cut he'd timed before, the held-breath thing, all circuits at once, forty-seven minutes since the last stop, give or take the thirty seconds he couldn't account for. David's mouth opened to say *interval* and Sarah's eyes went to the ceiling and Marcus was already counting under his breath because Marcus counted everything and never apologized, and the silence lasted maybe eight seconds, nine, and then it restarted mid-whir, as if it had simply been paused and somebody hit play.

And through the walkway glass, in the white wall sliding past like film through a projector, there was a shape standing at the tree line.

Because storms have edges, and roads have shoulders, and a shape needs ground under it, and none of that was visible — the world outside was solid white with texture moving through it, and the shape was standing in it the way a person stands in a room, squared to the building, arms down, at the distance where the drive disappeared. Not walking toward them. Walking anywhere. Just standing, facing the school, patient as a parked car.

"Don't move yet," Marcus said, and nobody had moved anyway, and he said it because he was the kind of man who gave orders even to statues. Then, to Jake, whose hands had gone up to the camera: "Not through glass. Not with flash. We don't know what reacts."

"Great, rules, love rules," Jake said, and lowered the camera, and did not lower the tripod, which was the closest thing to obeying anybody that had ever happened to him.

It took Marcus and David ninety seconds to get coats and boots and reach the inner door of the corridor along the walkway, and the whole ninety seconds David kept doing the math of distance — two hundred yards to the tree line, Marcus had counted it on arrival, and a shape at the tree line was two hundred yards away, and ninety seconds of walking a man at three miles an hour covers a football field, so if it had been coming, it would have halved the gap, and if it had been leaving, it would be gone, and neither of those things was true when the door opened, because the shape was not at the tree line anymore.

It was halfway. Maybe a hundred and twenty yards. Same stance, same squared shoulders, same arms down, and the white sliding past it so fast it blurred at the edges, and it had not walked, because the ground showed nothing, and it was not closer, because it was exactly where it had been when you looked at it directly, which is the sentence David wrote on the pad later, and then crossed out, because it wasn't true, and he wrote it again, because it was.

"Nobody chase it," Marcus said, at the glass, calm as a dispatcher. "Nobody opens the outer door. Elena, don't." Because she had, of course, started. "We watch until it quits."

It quit at minute fourteen, or it quit whenever they blinked, and David had the sense to start timing aloud and the bad luck to hear his own voice go flat and fast on the third count — *probability of direct observation failure at this distance in these conditions is maybe four percent, so either it's not four percent or we're not watching, in the likely case we're not watching properly, in the likely case the four percent is wrong, in the likely case—*

"David." Sarah, behind him, not touching him this time, just his name, the way you'd slam a laptop shut.

He stopped. The spot was empty. The white slid past. Marcus stood at the glass a long moment with his hands hanging loose at his sides, which was how you knew he'd been making himself stand like that, and then he said, to nobody, "it faced the building the whole time," and went back to get his notebook from his wife, because this was the kind of thing you wanted witnesses for, and the only witness he trusted was the one who wrote everything down.

They argued about it over lunch, which was canned soup heated on the folding stove because nobody had unpacked the good supplies and nobody was going to, and the argument was fine, the argument was healthy, the argument was exactly the sort of thing five adults with twenty years of missing memory needed: data. Sightings. Discrepancies. David laid out the three observations — tree line, halfway, gone — with timestamps, and Elena cross-checked against her map distances, and Marcus contributed the door-frame counts from the gallery so they could triangulate, and Sarah contributed the one thing nobody else could: she'd been staring at it the entire time, closer to the glass than anyone, and she said, careful, flat, visual, "the edges of it didn't match the snow. Everything else in that window loses detail the further it is. That thing kept its detail. It was like a photo taped over the window. It resolved when it shouldn't have."

Which is the sentence that broke David's method, and he broke it politely, the way you'd drop a glass in a library.

"Because resolution falls off with distance," he said, mostly to the pad. "That's not a rule of the school, that's a rule of optics. Atmosphere scatters light. If you're seeing something at a hundred and twenty yards with the clarity of something at twenty, then either the atmosphere is lying, or the distance is lying, or—" he turned the pen sideways, a kid's trick, buying a second — "or it isn't far. It's close, and it's pretending to be big. Which is a bigger problem than the ghost, because ghosts you can dismiss. Scale problems you can't dismiss, you can only re-measure, and I've measured it three times today, and my measurements disagree with each other, and that has never—"

He stopped. He hated the next clause, and he said it anyway, because he was built that way.

"—that has never once happened to me, in any system, and I need everybody to understand that I am not a man who gets two answers. I am a man who gets one answer and then finds out he was wrong later, like everybody. The later part hasn't arrived yet. When it arrives, I want it on the record that I already knew."

Silence, the good kind, the kind where five people are thinking the identical thought and won't say it first. Jake filled it, because Jake always filled it.

"So the weather report says the storm's a liar, the building's got a heartbeat, the coffee's haunted, and the guy outside is wearing distance like a coat." He raised his cup. "Honestly? Cleanest weekend I've had in years."

They laughed. It died mid-joke, theirs usually did now, but it got farther than most.

Night came the way it had been coming — early, total, the white outside turning gray and then black without ever becoming anything you could call dusk — and David had assigned himself the cot nearest the common room fire, which he justified on the basis of the thermometer (he slept cold, the fire helped, sound discipline) and which was a lie he told so smoothly even he almost bought it, because the reason was the knock. He wanted to be the first one awake when it came back. He wanted, more precisely, to be the one who caught it in the act of existing, because catching it meant a mechanism, and mechanisms could be reasoned with, and he had not slept at all since the window, and Sarah, who had, rolled over at one point and said into the dark, without opening her eyes, "stop trying to solve it before it happens," which was the most accurate diagnostic anyone had handed him in twenty years, and he said "okay," and did not.

It knocked at what his phone insisted was 2:03, and he chose to believe the phone, because belief was cheaper than the alternative.

Three knocks. The outer door of the covered walkway — he knew the sound instantly, the hollow boom of that metal, the way the corridor rang it like a bell, the difference between the inside door and the outside door, which was the difference between *someone visiting* and *something arriving.* Three knocks, patient, evenly spaced, the knuckles of a man who wasn't in a hurry and wasn't angry and wasn't attacking, and then a pause, and then three more.

Nobody spoke. Nobody moved. Marcus was up and at the gallery in four seconds barefoot, which meant he'd been asleep with his boots lined up, which meant he'd known, which meant none of them were surprised, they were only brave in the order they'd been born. Elena found Marcus's wrist in the dark without reaching for it. Sarah sat up and put both feet on the floor and waited, poised even in a borrowed sweater, waiting like a woman at an audition. Jake grabbed his jacket.

"Sit down," Marcus said, low, and Jake sat, mostly.

The knocking kept its rhythm. Three, pause, three, pause, three. No pounding, no variation, no escalation, no voice — nobody said *open up,* nobody said anything, and the absence of speech was somehow the friendliest part, because speech implied wants, and this thing had only a tempo. David found himself counting intervals instead of listening to fear, forty-one rows' worth of practice kicking in, and the intervals were perfect, machine-perfect, metronome-perfect, and then, at minute eleven, mid-rhythm — after a three, before the next one it owed — it stopped, and the stopping was not a fade and not a decision. It was a cut, the way the fans cut, the way the wind cut on the first night. Held breath. Out.

Silence held the room for a full minute after that, five people breathing audibly in the firelight, and then Jake said, very calm, the calm he wore like the camera vest:

"That's a person keeping time."

"No," Marcus said, still at the gallery door, not turning around, and the single syllable had the weight of a bolt thrown.

"That's a person, Marcus, knocking like a person, at a door, wanting in, and I've been in doorways at three a.m. all the years you people were sleeping with your shades drawn, and I know what that sounds like, because that's exactly what I sounded like, standing on porches with nothing in my pockets except a schedule—"

"Jake." Sarah, gentle, from the floor, both feet still down. "You sounded nothing like that. You pounded. You talked. You begged. That thing tonight didn't talk. Whatever's keeping time out there, it doesn't want in. It wants us to hear it. That's a different animal."

And that was the argument the night ended on, because nobody won, because you don't win an argument about whether a noise is a person — you just live in the answer, whichever way it falls — and Marcus, still facing the door, said finally, "everybody sleeps in one room tonight, fire's low, I'll feed it," which was the first time any of them had used the word *sleep* as a command instead of a hope, and they moved mattresses into the common room in the dark without arguing about positions, the five of them arranged around the dead fire like a meeting that had adjourned badly, and David lay on his cot with the legal pad under his pillow, forty-two rows now, and listened to the building hold its breath between events, and at some point after three he wrote, in the dark, without light, the last row he trusted for a week:

SYSTEM: visitor, outer door. OBSERVED: three knocks, eleven minutes, stopped mid-rhythm. EXPECTED: departed, or returned. DELTA: unknown.

And beneath it, in the margin, in the space where the truth would fit if the truth were a number:

*It kept its detail.*
