## Chapter Text

In the morning the thermos was full again, and this time nobody said a word about it, which was its own kind of report.

Jake got up before the others because he'd gotten good at it — at being the one awake, at watching a room full of people sleep like it was a contact sheet you could sort later. The common room looked different in gray light. The cots sat around the dead fire the way chairs sit after a meeting goes bad: close enough to pass a coffee, angled away from the door. Marcus slept on his back with his boots under the cot, toes to the opening, so a man didn't have to fumble in the dark. Elena slept curled around her notebook the way some women curl around a cat. Sarah slept flat and still, face relaxed, looking younger than she had any right to at thirty-eight, and David slept sitting up in the armchair like a student caught by a library, the legal pad under his pillow because of course it was under his pillow.

Jake had taken eleven photographs of them sleeping. He knew the number because he counted exposures the way Marcus counted doorframes, and he did not review a single one, because reviewing meant looking twice, and looking twice was how you noticed things, and there were, at present, plenty of ways to notice things without help.

That was the thing about the morning, though. It was organized. After the night of the knock the five of them had slept in a pile in the common room and then woken up and made coffee and started behaving like a crew, because behavior was cheaper than panic and everybody had a little of it left. Marcus assigned tasks the way he used to assign details. David kept a log of the assignments. Elena translated everything into words instead of maps. Sarah made breakfast on the folding stove with the competence of a woman who had catered three fashion weeks and refused, politely, to die of anything as undignified as hunger. And Jake volunteered for everything, which was new, and which nobody commented on except Sarah, who handed him an extra granola bar without being asked and said, "you're doing the thing where you keep busy so you don't have to stand still," and he said "thanks," because arguing with Sarah cost more than agreeing with her, and he went and stood perfectly still anyway, holding the granola bar, until she smiled and walked off.

"Corridor," Marcus said at nine, or what they'd agreed to call nine. "Residential to library, both ways. If the storm takes the walkway glass we lose the outside route, and if we've never proven the inside one, we don't have a plan B, we have a hope. I want it walked. Pairs."

He did not explain why he wanted it walked. That was the other change since the knock. Years ago, back when they were twenty and he was still trying to get into the academy, Marcus narrated everything — *I'm checking the lock because* — and Elena used to tease him about it for a week at a time. Now he just said the thing, and the thing happened, and the silence where the explanation used to be was the loudest weather report Jake had ever received.

Jake got paired with Elena. He would have preferred Marcus, who was quieter to walk beside, but Elena was the one who counted with her pen, and the one whose eyes had started going strange in the vaults, and there are worse people to have noticing a hallway with you.

They started from the residential end, because starting anywhere else meant trusting the building to tell you where you'd begun, and the residential corridor was the one place all five of them had walked on the first night, counting doors, finding the five made-up rooms. Fifty doors, Marcus had said. Two hundred yards, maybe, and then corrected himself to two hundred because he'd counted it, and Marcus trusted numbers he'd earned.

The corridor went on being exactly what a corridor should be. Doors on both sides, dark varnish, brass numbers polished like somebody buffed them weekly, nameplates over the lecture halls, chairs pushed in, lamps burning amber on the building's own timer, and the floor waxed to a shine that held their reflections upside down as they passed. Jake shot the floor once. He couldn't help it. A corridor with a reflection in it was a corridor that existed twice, and existing twice was, lately, a category worth documenting.

"Count with me," Jake said.

Elena uncapped her pen, thought better of it, capped it loose in her fist. "You count out loud. I'll write."

Which was how they discovered it, in the ordinary way these things are discovered — not with a scream, but with arithmetic.

Eleven, twelve, thirteen. The doors passed at the spacing they'd always passed, four feet of wall between frames, measured by stride the way locals taught locals. Fourteen, fifteen. Sixteen. Jake counted the way you breathe, mostly automatic, partly on purpose, and his stride was a photographer's stride, long and loose, and he knew what it cost him to cover ground because he'd spent a decade walking venues and hotels and porches and county lines, counting nothing and knowing everything by feel. So when seventeen arrived late — when the next doorframe failed to show up at the moment his legs expected it, and then showed up half a step further along than it had any business being — he stopped talking mid-number, and stood there, and let the silence finish the sentence.

"Door spacing's wrong," he said.

"It's not wrong," Elena said, and her voice had the careful flatness she saved for claims she intended to prove. "Spacing is identical. Look at the lamps. Even. Look at the doors. Even. What's wrong is the corridor. It's using the same furniture to make a longer room."

They kept walking. Jake counted, and the count kept arriving slower than the stride said it should, and Elena wrote the numbers down with the date, and neither of them said *we're moving slower*, because they weren't — same pace, same breathing, same conversation length, stretched out like taffy over a distance that refused to be finished. Twenty-four, twenty-five. Thirty-one. Forty. The library stair should have come up at the far end of the gallery, visible past the last dorm door, and it did, eventually, at the end of a stretch that took eleven minutes to cross, and Jake timed it, because David had taught everyone by example that timing things was how you stayed sane, and sanity was currently priced at whatever a stopwatch cost.

At the library landing they turned around, because the job was both ways, and Marcus's voice still sat in Jake's ear like a rule: *proven means walked twice, once each direction.*

Going back was shorter.

Not much shorter. Not dramatically — no lights flickering, nothing touching them. Just the doors arriving on time again, the stride covering what it should have covered, the whole corridor finishing itself in seven minutes instead of eleven. And that was the wrong direction of impossible, and they both knew it, because the natural horror is the road that grows when you leave and shrinks when you return, which is at least a story: something wants you far from home. But this corridor had grown when Jake walked it *toward* the library stair, toward the place where the dossiers were kept, toward the answer, and had shortened coming back, and nobody in the history of fear had built a haunted house that behaved like that, because haunted houses want you to run. This one had wanted him to arrive slowly. Or it had simply been longer while he was measuring it, which was somehow worse, because that meant the hallway had opinions about being watched.

Elena said none of this. She wrote it down, which was her version of saying it, and Jake watched her add *11 min / 7 min, same corridor, same pace* in the margin of the map she wasn't allowed to draw anymore, and then he did the only thing he had ever reliably known how to do with a feeling too large for a sentence.

He put the camera up. Tripod, level, straight down the center line of the corridor, back the way they'd come. One frame. No flash.

Through the viewfinder, the corridor was fifteen yards long.

He lowered the camera and the corridor was not fifteen yards long. The doors ran on to the bend where the junction swallowed sight, a good sixty, seventy yards at least, the lamps marching, the floor shining. He raised the camera again, because the human brain requires confirmation of its betrayals, and the viewfinder gave it: short. Clean. The library stair at the end of a tiny, ordinary hallway, exactly the size it had been the first night they'd walked it, exactly the size two hundred yards should look when it is telling the truth.

"Look at this," Jake said, and his voice came out the way it did at auditions and auction houses and doorways — professional, unhurried, the tone that meant *I am not okay and I am handling it.* He handed her the camera without waiting for permission, which was itself remarkable, because Jake Mercer had not handed anyone a camera since the yearbook party, since the accident, since the beginning of the long move around the country that had ended in a dormitory with five made-up beds.

Elena looked through the viewfinder. Then she looked at the corridor. Then she looked through the viewfinder again, the way she read ledger lines — sideways, then straight, then sideways, the motion of a person checking whether the document lied or whether she did.

"The picture," she said slowly, "remembers the hallway being normal."

"Yeah."

"That's not possible."

"Yeah."

She handed the camera back, and here is the thing Jake carried out of that corridor and did not say until much later, when saying it cost him more: she had looked *through* the viewfinder first, the way a person looks when they expect the world to match the machine. Most people, shown a wrong photograph, check the photograph. Elena had checked the world, and found it wanting, and written the discrepancy down without asking either of them to fix it. Fifteen years of marriage will do that to your aim.

They walked back to the common room at a jog, counting, and the corridor gave them the long version again on the way out and the short version on the way in, as if it had never done anything else, and Jake's count matched Elena's count and neither matched the eleven-and-seven, and the numbers disagreed with each other in the polite, permanent way David's had yesterday. By the time they came through the doors, Marcus was standing at the table with David's pad open in his big hands, forty-two rows of a man's method laid out like a map of somebody's nerves.

Because Jake had left his tally sheet on the table that morning — exposure counts, times, the eleven-minute and the seven-minute, written in pencil because pencil was honest — and David had found it, and David did with other people's data what David did with all data, which was enter it, and the entered rows now sat in the DELTA column with a note in the handwriting that had gotten smaller as the entries got stranger: *same corridor, two distances, both true, mechanism unknown.*

"You felt it too," Marcus said. Not a question.

"It stretches," Jake said. "Going in. Comes back normal. And the—" He stopped. Started again, because a man who'd spent eleven months learning to say hard things in rooms that hadn't burned down yet could at least finish a sentence in front of his friends. "And the camera doesn't see the extra. Whatever the extra is, it doesn't develop."

Silence, the kind where five people think the identical thought and won't say it first, and then Sarah, practical, handing him the exact cost of continuing: "So the hallway gets longer when we walk it, but not when it's photographed. Which means it isn't the hallway." She looked at the dead fire. "It's us walking it."

Nobody answered that, because answering it meant choosing which flavor of terrible you preferred, and lunch was canned soup again, and the folding stove hissed, and the day went on being a day with the stubbornness of weather.

Evening came early and total, the white outside turning gray and then black without ever becoming anything you could call dusk, and Jake set the camera up one last time on the low table near the fire and shot the five of them the way they actually sat: unposed, mugs in laps, Marcus facing the door out of habit, Elena writing, David reading his own pad like an indictment, Sarah looking at the fire like it owed her money. He fired once, lit by the amber lamps and nothing else, and reviewed it, because it was the last frame of the day and someone should know the room had been real.

The photograph showed all five of them, and the corridor mouth behind them, and the doors on either side of the gallery, and the spacing was even, and the lamps marched, and the hallway in the picture was exactly, faithfully, unbearably normal.

Jake put the camera down and looked at the actual room, which was also normal, which was the problem, which was the problem, and Marcus said "eat your soup, Mercer," and he ate his soup, and his hands were steady.

They had been steady all day. He hadn't noticed until now, sitting in the firelight with the negative of a stretching hallway somewhere in his vest like evidence in a case nobody had filed, that he'd held a camera through a corridor that changed its mind and a metronome knock and a man-shaped hole in a storm, and the fine shake that had lived in his shooting hand since he was nineteen — the one the doctors called constitutional, the one that had quietly cost him what was left of his studio work in a darkroom in Portland, the one that made every photograph he took a small act of faith — had not shown up for work.

Hang on. He set the cup down and looked at his own hands the way other men look at a bill they don't remember signing. Eleven months of meetings had given him back most of what the years had taken, but not this. Not the shake. He'd switched to film partly because digital autofocus couldn't hold a subject when his hands couldn't, had bought the lightest body he could find, had braced every elbow against a wall or a car roof or his own ribs, and the whole time, in the back of every frame, the old bargain ran: the pictures stay sharp somehow, so somebody keeps paying for them.

Whatever was paying for it out here, he did not intend to file a complaint. He did not say anything about it, either. Some rows weren't his. But he lay on his cot afterward listening to the building hold its breath between events, thinking about a hallway that grew when you approached it and a camera that remembered it being normal, and the thought he couldn't shake wasn't a question about the school. It was the old one, the one underneath all of them, the one he'd carried through every rehab intake form and every rented room with a view of somebody else's parking lot: if the picture stays the same and the world keeps changing, which one of them is the record?

And outside, at the edge of the grounds, past the glass, past the walkway, past the tree line where the drive disappeared, the storm kept its own hours, and the wind cut — not down, not gradually, but *off*, mid-breath, the way the fans cut, the way the knocking cut, and in the second of true silence that followed, if any of them had been awake at the window they might have heard what the quiet sounded like.

It sounded like a hallway, waiting to be walked.
