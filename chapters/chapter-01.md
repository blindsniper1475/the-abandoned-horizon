## Chapter Text

The blizzard was not weather so much as a wall — a physical weight of white that pressed against the windshield of the all-terrain truck and erased the world beyond the glass. There was no horizon, no road ahead, only the slow sweep of the wipers carving brief tunnels through the falling snow before the white closed over them again. Inside the cab, the air was a thick soup of stale coffee, old leather, and the sharp metallic breath of a heater losing its argument with Alaska.

Marcus drove. He always drove — not because anyone had asked him to, but because he was the kind of man who trusted his own hands on a wheel more than he trusted anyone else's. His knuckles were loose on the leather, his eyes steady in the mirror of the road behind them, which had already vanished. Beside him, Elena sat in the passenger seat with her wool-lined parka still buttoned to the chin, a notebook open on her knee and a pen capped in her fist. She wasn't writing. When Elena wasn't writing, Marcus had learned in twenty years of marriage, it meant she was worried.

In the back, three figures were wedged between crates and duffel bags that rose nearly to the ceiling of the truck. Sarah sat in the middle, poised and still, her dark coat brushed free of snow, her eyes on the white nothing outside the side window. David was pressed against the far door, his smartphone held up in one gloved hand, thumbing through weather radar that showed nothing but a solid bruise of purple over the entire county. Jake sat nearest the cab, his camera bag strapped across his chest even indoors, his long frame folded around it like a man protecting a sleeping child.

The truck itself was a beast — a heavy-duty, four-door all-terrain rig lifted on oversize tires, with a roof rack and a cargo bed that Marcus had spent two weeks packing. Under the tarps and behind the seats lay crates of canned goods, sealed water, dehydrated meals, batteries, fuel canisters, a folding stove, first-aid kits, and a hand-crank radio. Three months of supplies, give or take. They had argued about the amount at first — Sarah had wanted two weeks, "enough to wait out any storm" — but Marcus had pushed for more. He remembered the winters of his childhood, how a storm could pin a town in place for a month, sometimes two, sometimes longer. His grandmother had lost a season once to a blizzard that simply refused to end. So they had packed for three months, and the truck had sagged on its springs, and nobody had complained.

They had known, too, that the school was abandoned. Fifteen years since anyone had lived there — since the foundation had pulled its staff and sealed the doors and let the place sit at the edge of the sea like a monument to something nobody wanted to name. Knowing that, they had packed as if for a siege: because the building had no working stores, no deliveries, no neighbors. Whatever they had brought, they had.

"Radar's useless," David said, tilting the phone so the pale screen caught the cab light. "Solid cell. It's not letting up for days. Maybe a week." He paused, reading something else on the screen — pressure readings, maybe, or the wind alerts he had been refreshing since Fairbanks. "They're saying this one could sit."

"Good thing we brought enough," Jake said, from somewhere behind the crates.

"Good thing *Marcus* brought enough," Sarah corrected, quietly. "I voted for two weeks."

"You voted for two weeks because you didn't want to ride with a month of canned peaches," Elena said, without turning around, and a ripple of laughter moved through the cab — brief, human, warm.

Marcus allowed himself a small smile, but his eyes stayed on the white.

The road had stopped being a road twenty minutes ago. It was a suggestion now — a memory of asphalt that surfaced occasionally under the snow, cracked and heaved into low ridges by decades of freezing and thawing. The temperature swings up here were brutal: fifty degrees in summer, sixty below in winter, and the ground had taken it personally. The pavement was a patchwork — old asphalt breaking apart into gravel, gravel packed into ruts, ruts filled with black ice. Every half mile the truck bucked and lurched, tires grinding over fissures that sent jolts up through the frame. The crates in the back shifted and thumped with each impact. Marcus adjusted the wheel, feathered the gas, and let the truck find its own way over the broken ground.

The radio had been on since Anchorage — a local station, low-powered and crackling, mostly a talk format where a host with a tired voice read weather bulletins between calls from listeners describing roads that no longer existed. *Route 3 to the coast is closed indefinitely,* the host was saying now, in the easy monotone of a man who had said it before and would say it again. *National Guard's staging in Delta Junction, but they're not coming north until this breaks. If you're heading toward the coast, don't. If you're already there — well. You know where the shelters are.* A pause. The soft clink of a coffee cup against a saucer. *Bundle up, folks. This one's going to be a while.*

The voice was the last thread of the ordinary world — a man in a warm studio somewhere, sipping coffee, talking to strangers. Marcus listened to it the way a diver might listen to the last few seconds of air.

"Twenty years," Elena said at last, and the words were barely audible under the heater's roar. "It feels like we were just in these halls. Kids. Yesterday."

*It doesn't,* Marcus thought, but he didn't say it. He remembered being twenty — he remembered the weight of it, the noise, the way the future had felt like a room with no walls. Now the future felt like this road: a white nothing with a truckload of canned food in it and four people who had each independently decided that the most important thing in the world was to go back to a building everyone else had forgotten.

He thought about the letter. Alex's letter, arriving out of nowhere six weeks ago — thick cream paper, handwritten, no return address, the postmark smeared. *The school is reopening. One night. Come.* A class of sixty, and only five of them had written back. Five out of sixty. Marcus had read Elena's reply before she sealed it — just a date, a time, and a single line: *We'll be there.* He had driven them north in a truck packed like a lifeboat because some old, wordless part of him had understood, before his conscious mind did, that this was not an invitation. It was a summons.

*I shouldn't have come,* he thought, and the thought arrived old and worn, like something he'd been carrying in his pocket for years. *I should have stayed in Texas. I should have stayed where the road was straight and the lines were clear.* But he looked at Elena — at the set of her jaw, the pen still capped, the notebook still empty — and he knew he would have come anyway. He would have driven off any cliff she pointed at. Twenty years of marriage had taught him that much about himself.

"I keep thinking about the gym floor," Sarah said suddenly, from the back. Her voice was low, almost a murmur, as if she were talking to herself and had simply forgotten there were others in the cab. "The way the light came through the high windows in the morning. Gold on the varnish. I used to sit on the bleachers before class and watch it move across the floor." She paused. "I don't remember what I was thinking about. But I remember the light."

"You were thinking about Danny Okafor," Jake said. "You were in love with Danny Okafor."

"I was *aware* of Danny Okafor," Sarah said, with great dignity, and the cab erupted — laughter, overlapping denials, David demanding to know who Danny Okafor was, Jake describing him in lavish and unflattering detail. For a moment it was not five adults driving into a blizzard toward an abandoned school; it was five kids in a truck, and the years folded up and put themselves away.

Marcus drove. The heater roared. The radio voice read another bulletin. And the road, such as it was, carried them north.

Then the school appeared — not emerging from the fog so much as resolving into it, the way a photograph develops in a chemical bath.

Twelve brutalist towers of grey concrete and dark glass, packed inside four acres at the very edge of the sea, fused together by enclosed walkways into a single sprawling body. It was a fortress: high walls, few windows on the lower floors, everything designed as much to keep things in as to keep things out. The concrete was clean — too clean for a building that had stood empty for fifteen years. No graffiti. No boarded windows. The snow had been cleared from the courtyard in a perfect rectangle, as if someone had swept it that morning.

"It looks like a photograph," Sarah said, and her voice had gone strange and quiet.

Marcus killed the engine. For a moment nobody moved. The only sound was the tick of cooling metal and the distant, directionless moan of the wind.

Then Jake opened his door, and the cold came in like something with teeth.

They climbed out and stood together in the courtyard — five adults in heavy winter gear, ringed by the towers. Marcus pulled his insulated jacket tighter and looked up. The buildings rose on every side, dark windows reflecting the white sky. The snow should have been drifting against the walls after fifteen years of storms. It wasn't. The courtyard was swept clean down to the grey stone.

Elena was writing now, fast, her pen moving across the page in short strokes. Marcus watched her for a moment, then shouldered the first crate and led them toward the main entrance.

Inside, the heat was on.

That was the first wrongness, though none of them named it yet. The air inside the entrance hall was warm — not the stale, wrapped-in-clothing warmth of a sealed building, but the live, moving warmth of a heating system running on a timer. The lights were on: not emergency strips or battery lamps, but full, warm overhead light that made the long hallway glow amber and pushed the corners back into clean shadow. The floors had been waxed recently enough to hold their shine. The air smelled of floor wax and old paper and the faint, institutional ghost of floor cleaner.

"It's been maintained," David said. He had put his phone away — there was no signal here anyway — and was standing very still, looking down the length of the hall. "Somebody's been maintaining this place."

"Probably the foundation," Sarah said. "Sent a crew out before we arrived. Alex's people."

"That would explain the snow," Elena said, and kept writing.

Marcus set the crate down and counted heads without meaning to. Five. He always counted. Then he looked down the long, straight hallway — doors on both sides, identical, receding into warm light — and felt something settle in his chest that was not quite relief.

They found the common room on the ground floor of the central tower: a wide, high-ceilinged space with deep chairs arranged around a stone fireplace, bookshelves lining the walls, and a fire already laid and — impossibly — already lit. Someone had struck a match within the last hour. The flames were low and steady, feeding on dry hardwood.

Nobody mentioned it. They all saw it, and nobody mentioned it.

Jake crossed to the fireplace and knelt by the mantel, where a row of framed photographs still stood — the school's own, dusted and straightened, class composites from decades when the halls had been loud. He studied them the way he studied everything, head tilted, patient.

"It's like nobody ever left," he said finally. "No dust on the frames. You'd think after fifteen years..." He let it trail off and instead unzipped the camera bag at his side and drew out a fat manila envelope, and from the envelope a stack of photographs — real prints, 4x6, glossy, slightly curled at the corners.

"I found these at my mom's house," he said, fanning them across the coffee table. "Our senior year. I scanned them last month and had them printed at the drugstore, like it was 1999."

There they were. Sixteen-year-old Sarah on the bleachers, chin on her knees, the gym floor gold beneath her. David at a computer terminal, grinning at something off-camera with the unguarded brightness of a boy who had not yet learned to be careful with his face. Elena in the library, pen raised mid-sentence, caught in the act. Marcus in a flannel shirt almost identical to the one he wore now, squinting into the flash.

"Who took these?" Sarah asked, picking one up.

"Alex," Jake said. "Alex took all of them. That's why he's never in any of them."

The name sat briefly in the warm air between them. In every photograph, the photographer was missing — the absent eye behind every shot, the one face the collection could not contain. Marcus looked at the spread of them on the table: his children, really, though none of them would have used the word. The faces he had known before mortgages and mileage and the long, slow education of getting older.

David turned over a photograph of the computer lab — banks of beige towers, cable trays overhead. "They ripped all of this out when they closed," he said. "I checked the auction records a few years back. Everything sold for scrap." He tapped the image. "Twelve towers, and that was one room. Do you know how much infrastructure it took to run this place?"

"More than they told us, apparently," Elena said, and her pen kept moving.

Sarah gathered the photographs into a neat stack, squared the edges, and set them along the mantel above the fire with the care of someone handling something holy. "We should keep them out," she said. "There's no reason for them to live in an envelope."

They shed their coats, and the layers underneath became visible for the first time since the drive: Marcus in a flannel shirt over a thermal, Elena in a heavy wool sweater with her notebook now finally open, Sarah in a cashmere turtleneck and jeans, David in a fleece pullover with his phone back in his hand almost unconsciously, Jake in a worn canvas jacket over a camera vest bristling with lens caps and spare batteries. They looked, Marcus thought, like people who had dressed for a weekend outdoors and ended up somewhere else entirely.

They sat. Elena found a tin of coffee in the kitchen — still good, sealed, dated eighteen months ahead — and boiled water on the stove, and they passed around chipped ceramic mugs and talked.

They talked the way people talk when they have been apart too long and know it: quickly, in layers, skipping the boring parts. Marriages — Marcus and Elena, solid and weathered; Sarah and David, once, briefly, years ago, a fact they now discussed with the cheerful detachment of archaeologists examining a dig. Jake, who had never once stayed anywhere. Careers built and abandoned. Parents buried. Children raised in other states, other time zones, lives of their own. Twenty years compressed into ninety minutes of firelit conversation, and underneath it all, the thing nobody said out loud: that a class of sixty had received a letter, and only five chairs had been filled.

Marcus sipped his coffee and let the voices wash over him, and thought: *We're here. We actually came.*

He looked at each of them in turn — Sarah's still face in the firelight, David's restless hands, Jake's easy sprawl, Elena's quick, watchful eyes — and felt the old weight of them, the old, wordless loyalty that had survived twenty years of distance because it had never been about distance. It was about this: five people who had seen each other before the world taught them to be careful, and had never entirely forgiven the world for it.

"It's like we never left," Marcus said, and believed it, and looked around at the only people he had ever really trusted.

Later — after the fire had burned low and the radio had gone to static and the storm outside had deepened from a roar into a steady, animal pressure against the walls — Elena stopped talking mid-sentence.

She was looking down the hallway.

A light at the far end — thirty yards away, near the door to the east wing — had stuttered. Not dimmed. *Stuttered.* A single, sharp blink, and in the blink, for one frame that Marcus's eye refused to hold onto, the light had been the wrong color: a brief, chemical green, like phosphor on an old screen.

"Did you see that?" Elena said.

Marcus had seen it. He felt it in his teeth, the way you feel a dropped plate before you hear it.

"Old wiring," he said, and his voice had an edge in it that he did not intend and could not remove. "It's a fifteen-year-old building. The ballasts are shot. It's settling."

"It's not settling, Marcus," David said. He was very still. "That's not what settling looks like."

"It looks like a bad lightbulb," Marcus said, "and we're tired, and we drove nine hours through a white-out, and I'd like to finish my coffee."

A beat of silence. Then Jake chuckled — low, easy, unhurried — and raised his mug. "To bad lightbulbs," he said, and the tension broke, and they laughed, and Elena capped her pen, and the fire popped, and the warmth held.

They settled back into the chairs. The hallway was quiet and straight and full of amber light, and the light at the far end did not flicker again.

But Marcus kept his eyes on it.

And out past the walls, the white-out had stopped sounding like weather. It hummed now, low and even, like something breathing through its teeth — and somewhere in the long, straight halls of the school, a man in a maintenance coat put his hand on a ring of keys and began, again, to hope.
