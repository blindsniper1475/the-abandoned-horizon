## Chapter Text

The storm had not let go by morning, and nobody had expected it to. Sarah woke in the made-up room at seven-something — she did not check, because nothing down there kept time she trusted — alone, with the lamp already burning, and her coffee gone cold on the nightstand beside a ring pressed into the wood where the mug had sat steaming for longer than it took her to wake. The thermos stood next to it, unmarked, unscrewed, poured to the exact line she drank it to. David's side of the room was made up tight, the way he left it every morning of their marriage, and there was no sign he had slept at all. Down the gallery she could hear them talking near the dead fire in the common room — David's flat careful voice braided with Elena's quicker one, going over what they had found the day before, names and numbers pitched too low from this distance to carry. He had probably carried the cup up for her sometime in the night, before whatever this conversation was started, and set it out like room service, and gone back down to keep the fire company. She did not ask him about it. There were six parts of the morning to bring up downstairs instead, and this was the least useful one.

The common room smelled of the fire having been rebuilt at some point in the night — fresh kindling smell under the old smoke — and Marcus was already up, doing something with a length of cord and a chair leg that he would not have been able to explain if asked. Elena had her notebook out and her pen capped; she and David had clearly broken off mid-sentence when Sarah came in, because the silence they handed her was a specific shape, though neither of them looked guilty enough to have earned it. Jake was asleep sitting up in the big chair with his camera on his chest the way other men sleep with a hand over their hearts, and nobody had the heart or the nerve to wake him.

"Records," Elena said, when Sarah sat down. "That's what I want to find. Not the library's circulation book — we saw that. The school's own records. Admissions, class lists, staff. If a place this size ran for forty years somebody filed something somewhere."

"Basement," Marcus said, without looking up from the cord. "Service stairs off the library. Back when they built it, they ran everything underground because the ground stays warm past thirty below. You could heat a whole floor off one pipe." He said it the way he said things he'd read on a plaque somewhere and stored, and Sarah thought, not for the first time, that Marcus had been collecting facts about this place for twenty years without admitting he was collecting them.

They went down after coffee. The service stairs were behind a door marked STAFF ONLY in paint so old the letters had begun to flake, and they spiraled — Sarah counted the turns because counting gave her hands something to do — three floors' worth of spiral into a level the directory on the wall had never admitted existed. The air got warmer going down, not colder, which was wrong, and then stopped being wrong and started being simply the way it was, which was worse somehow, and by the time they reached the bottom the noise of the storm was gone entirely and the silence had a texture to it, like the hum of a held breath.

The vaults were lower than the rest of the school. Ceilings maybe eight feet, painted institutional cream, lit by fixtures that threw no shadows — flat, even, sourceless light that came from everywhere and nowhere, and Sarah noticed it the way she noticed a photographer's softbox: professionally, and with a small chill, because you paid a great deal of money to make light behave like that and nobody here had needed to pay anything at all. Rows of metal cabinets ran in aisles, each cabinet taller than Marcus, each drawer labeled in the same upright hand she had seen in the library ledger. Index drawers. Card catalogues. Class records, sorted by year, the years running down the spine of the building like tree rings.

Elena found their graduation year first because Elena always found the thing first, and she said the number aloud — *Nineteen ninety-nine through two thousand and six* — and then found the box, and then they were all leaning over it like kids at a treasure chest, and the warmth of it took the room by surprise.

There were photographs. That was the first thing. Photographs pinned to index cards with brass clips: the five of them at eighteen, squinting, stealing each other's hats, Marcus caught mid-laugh with his mouth full, Elena throwing something at him with excellent aim. A teacher named Mr. Sorensen standing in front of a blackboard with his back turned, caught in the act of writing the same word over and over — *retention*, chalked out in tall schoolmaster capitals down the whole width of the board, which nobody commented on because they were looking at the caption — *Mr. Sorensen, senior seminar, final lecture* — and remembering, all five of them at once, that they did not remember Mr. Sorensen at all.

"God," Jake said, gently, the way you'd say it over a grave. "Look at us. Look at my hair."

"You had better hair than everyone," Sarah said, and heard how flat her own voice came out, and tried again. "You had the best hair. I remember thinking it wasn't fair."

"That's true," Jake said, pleased. "That's actually true."

They went through the box the way people go through a shoebox of old letters — slowly, and then all at once. Pranks. A suspended bus route. A girl named Ada Kowalski who had thrown a clock into the boiler and been expelled for it and whose card, at the back, bore a photograph taken later — a woman of forty-something standing on a porch in Tucson holding a mug, wearing the exact expression Ada must have worn at eighteen when the clock hit the water. Somebody had taken that picture. Somebody had brought it back here and clipped it to the card.

"Okay," David said, carefully, the way you say *okay* to a dog you're not sure about. "What's the second drawer."

It wasn't a question, exactly. It was too neat to be a question. The second drawer was deeper, and the tabs inside it weren't sorted by graduation year but by name, and every single name in the school's history was in there, filed alphabetically, and beside each tab was a folder, and the folders were thick, and the thickness of them was the problem, because the students in these folders had graduated twenty years ago and nobody should have been following them since.

Down here the little ache behind Elena's eyes had come back — the one from the greenhouse, the one she hadn't told anybody about — and she blinked it away like a dust mote and said nothing, because saying nothing was also a technique. Elena pulled hers out before anyone could stop her, and then handed it around, because that was how she worked — she could not keep a document to herself any more than Marcus could keep a hazard uncounted.

FELLS, JONAS R. Born. Graduated. Height, weight, blood type. *Relationship status: married (19yrs), two children.* Address in Fairbanks. Occupation: heavy-equipment mechanic, then union steward, then a line for *unemployed, current*, updated in pencil in a date that was last month's date. Notable events: a truck accident, a divorce filing dropped, a brother's death, a DUI dismissed. Family ties listed down the margin like a pedigree chart. And at the top right corner, typed, alone, a figure: **88%**.

"That's our Jonas," Marcus said, and his voice did something complicated. "Jonas Fells. He sat behind us in thermo-dynamics and cheated off Elena's paper every final and got a better grade anyway." He turned the folder over, looked at the blank back, turned it again. "Eighty-eight. What's eighty-eight."

"A number," Elena said, and wrote it in her notebook. She did not underline it. She wrote it down the way she wrote down everything, which was the worst possible reaction, because it meant she had decided it mattered and was going to let the rest of them catch up on their own.

MARCUS W. HAYES. Married fifteen years. Texas. An address Elena read aloud and stopped reading aloud halfway through because it was their address, complete, including the unit number of the quarters on the Ranger compound, which had been reassigned twice since they moved in. *Occupation: law enforcement, state service, 20yrs continuous.* Notable events: a wreck on the Dalton Highway, a commendation, a bar fight in Amarillo three winters back that ended with a sentence reading *no further action* — which had never appeared in any precinct report Marcus had ever been shown, because there had never been a report. Family ties: *spouse; one son, b. 2013 — summer residence, camp, TX.* Sarah watched Marcus look at his own son written down as a line item and watched him put the folder back in the drawer face-down, which was the loudest thing he had ever done with his hands.

DAVID KELLER. Recruited out of high school, the folder said, and then a company name none of them recognized, and then a list of clearances. *Relationship status: married (5 yrs).* Oregon. An address. A mortgage balance, current to the month. Notable events: a promotion, a lawsuit against an insurer dropped, a therapy referral completed. *Family ties: spouse; mother, deceased 2019.* Beside the name: **79%**. David stared at it the way you stare at a bill for something you didn't buy. "I've never told anybody about the therapy referral," he said, mildly, to the cabinet. "Not even Sarah."

"I know," Sarah said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it.

ELENA M. HAYES. Her career in lines — bylines collected like specimens, an award nobody mentioned at the wedding, a freelance contract terminated *subject declined assignment, ongoing*, and beneath that a column headed simply *notable*, containing a single entry: *survived exposure event, site unknown, age 16*. Then the number: **73%**, the lowest in the row, and Sarah saw Elena look at it and write it down and cap her pen, which was how you knew she had finished with a fact, and Sarah saw the jaw-muscle jump that meant she was choosing not to ask what *exposure event* meant yet, because Elena saved questions up like ammunition.

SARAH L. KELLER. Then her own, handed to her by Jake with a little ceremony that failed to be funny, and she opened it because refusing would have been a scene and scenes required energy.

Married five years. Oregon. Same address as David's, mirrored, cross-referenced — the folders linked to each other with little penciled arrows like a conspiracy board. *Occupation: modeling (retired), brand consulting, patronage.* Notable events: a broken wrist, a feature film cameo, a bankruptcy she had never told anyone about, a foundation she ran anonymously with David's money and her name off the paperwork. And then, at the top, clipped to the tab with a brass clip like all the others, a photograph of her at nineteen — standing in a doorway she did not recognize, wearing a coat she did not remember owning, looking at the camera with an expression she had not known she could make. It was shot well. Terribly well. Whoever shot it knew exactly what they were doing, and it was not a snapshot, and it was not candid, and it was not taken by anyone who loved her.

Beneath the photo, a line: *Recruitment candidate, image asset retained, age 19.* And then her number: **84%**.

She turned the page. There were more pages. There were photographs of her at twenty-two, twenty-six, thirty-one, thirty-five — annual, professional, each one better lit than the last, each one showing a woman getting more famous for being looked at while getting less recognizable to herself. She had never seen any of them. She had never been offered any of them. On the back of the last one, in the same upright hand as the ledger, someone had written *holds well*, and underlined *well*, and Sarah put the folder down with both hands, the way you set something on a table when your fingers have started telling you things your eyes don't want confirmed.

"They're all photographed," she said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of it and sickened by it in the same breath. "Every folder has photographs. Who shoots these? People aren't famous enough to have photographers following them around, and I guarantee you I would have noticed one."

"You would notice," David agreed, gently, because David had met her at nineteen and had spent five years learning that Sarah noticed everything and reported nothing until it couldn't wait anymore. "You notice cameras. You notice lenses. You would have noticed a lens pointed at you for twenty years."

"Then why didn't I?"

Nobody answered that, and the answer to it was the reason nobody answered it, and the silence had a shape in it, and the shape was a person with a camera nobody could see.

"Maybe they were famous," David said, in his log voice, offering a door nobody wanted to walk through. "Plenty of people get photographed for a living and never see the proofs. Agencies keep the negatives, not the talent."

"That's a comfort," Elena said. "Look at Jonas. He drove a loader for thirty years. Who shoots a loader driver on a porch in Tucson?"

David didn't have an answer for that one, which was how you knew he'd already run every possible answer and kept only the ones that held water, and none of them did.

Jake's folder was in the drawer between David's and Elena's, tabbed MERCER, J., and it was thicker than all the others, and Sarah closed the drawer over it without opening it, and then opened it again, and then said, very quietly, "I'm not reading your mail," and Jake said, "Appreciate it, man," and neither of them moved for a moment, and then Jake picked the folder up himself, weighed it in his hands, and put it back, and shut the drawer, and that was the most afraid Sarah had ever seen him, and he hadn't touched the contents.

At the end of the run of names there was one more tab, and it was wider than the others, and the folders behind it were not one folder but a stack — dozens, bound with linen tape, each one dated, the dates running backward from now to graduation and then beyond graduation to the year the school opened, and the first tab in the stack read VOSS, ALEXANDER T., and Elena pulled it, and opened it, and the photograph that fell out onto the cabinet lid was Alex at eighteen, standing in front of the twelve towers with a bag over his shoulder, squinting into a sun that the weather hadn't shown in nine days, and on the back of the photo, in handwriting every one of them recognized now because they had all seen it somewhere else, in the library, on the ledger, on the reunion letter: *still here.*

The folder's occupation line read *CUSTODIAN, SITE ARCHIVE, 20YRS CONTINUOUS*. Its relationship status line was blank. Its address was this building. Its notable events were a list of dates — dozens of them, recent ones, older ones — each followed by a single word that meant nothing to any of them, a technical term that might have been a piece of plumbing or a piece of theology, and its family ties section said *none recorded*, and the number in the top right corner was **100%**, and it was the only number in the entire drawer that had been printed in red ink, and the red was fresh, the way ink is fresh when it has been re-dated recently enough to still sit on top of the paper rather than sink into it.

"He's been in here the whole time," Marcus said, finally, in the voice he used for hazard signs. "Twenty years. Every folder in this drawer gets updated and his is the only one that doesn't have an address."

"It does," Elena said, and pointed, because the address line was filled in. It just said *SITE*. "He lives here. He's worked here twenty years. He mailed sixty letters and five showed up and he filed all five of us before we got here, and he filed himself last, and he updates his own folder—" She stopped. She flipped a page. She flipped another. "This one's dated yesterday."

Nobody said anything for a while after that. Down in the flat light, with the storm gone silent overhead and the cabinets standing in their rows like something planted, the five of them stood around a drawer of paper that described their lives better than they could describe them themselves, and Sarah kept looking at the photograph of herself at nineteen in the doorway she didn't remember, taken by a man she had never met, in a building that had kept her sharper than she had kept herself, and she thought, quite calmly, the way you think when the ground goes out under something and you have a few seconds before the fall: *I am the only one of us who looks like me in that picture, and I don't know why they wanted me.*

"We take these upstairs," Marcus said. It wasn't loud. It was the voice he used when he'd already decided and was giving everyone else permission to agree. "All of them. We take them up, we lay them out, and we read every page, and then we find whoever's been keeping this drawer, and we ask them." He looked at the red number, at the tabbed stack bound in linen, at the photo of Alex Voss squinting into a sun that wasn't there anymore. "Somebody's been keeping this place's books. Might as well meet the bookkeeper."

They gathered the folders — all of them, every tabbed life in the drawer, stacked and carried up the spiral stairs in two arms apiece, Elena's notebook pressed flat against her chest with the numbers copied out and the pen capped and the question not yet written down — and the service door swung shut behind them with a sound that was almost polite, and the vaults settled back into their flat light, empty except for one drawer left open, and inside it, one folder lying face-up on the cabinet lid, its photograph corner catching the beam.

It was Sarah's. At nineteen. In the doorway.

On the cabinet beside it, where nobody would have thought to look up from carrying armfuls of their own lives, a small brass plate read ABSCISSA INDUSTRIES, PROPERTY OF, and beneath that, in the same upright hand as everything else, freshly, still wet: *they read well.*
