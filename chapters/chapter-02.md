## Chapter Text

Nobody moved for a moment after the flicker. The fire had burned down to a lattice of orange coals, and the common room had gone the color of old newsprint, and all five of them were looking down the hallway at the place where the light had failed — thirty yards off, near the east-wing door — where the amber now sat steady and innocent, as if it had never done anything but burn. "Bad lightbulb," Marcus had said. Jake had raised his mug to it. But sitting in chairs with a laugh still drying on their faces, none of them could make the phrase sound true a second time.

"I'm not sleeping in this room," Elena said.

It came out in her reporting voice — flat, factual, already written down — and she saw Marcus register it the way he always did: a small tightening at the corner of his jaw, the calculation of an argument he wasn't going to win.

"There are dormitories in the residential wing," she went on. "Beds, doors that close. If we're staying — and we are staying, because the road out is under nine inches of snow and it will be under it for days — then we're staying in rooms with doors."

"Seconded," David said, without looking up from his phone. The screen threw his face into hard blue relief. "There's no signal, no Wi-Fi, and a light just did something lights don't do. I'd like a door."

"Rooms with doors," Marcus agreed. He stood, and the chair made a sound on the waxed floor like a page being turned. "We stay together, we find the beds, we come back for the crates in the morning. Nobody wanders."

"Nobody wanders," Jake echoed, slinging his camera bag across his chest. He said it the way he said everything — pleasantly, as if it were a phrase he'd heard once in a film and found charming.

Sarah folded her scarf into her bag with three precise movements and stood. "Which way?"

Elena looked at her map. She had drawn it in the common room over the first hour — the entrance hall, the corridor they'd come in by, the tower they were sitting in now — and it was already an act of faith. The school had been closed for fifteen years. There were no plans online, no campus directory, nothing in her fifteen years of research files except a mailing address and a photograph of the front gate in summer. So the map was her own, drawn from memory that was twenty years old and from a building she had never actually been permitted to roam. She had been a student here. That was the part she could never quite reconcile: she had walked these halls for four years, and she could not remember where anything was.

"East," she said. "The residential wing shares a covered walkway with the academic block. If the old campus map in the yearbook is right —"

"You drew the old campus map in the yearbook," Marcus said.

"I redrew it. From memory. Which is not the same thing, and we all know it."

They went out through the entrance hall, five coats over five arms, the fire behind them winking at their backs. The building received them the way deep water receives a stone — without sound, without ripple. Their boots found the polished concrete of the main corridor, and their footsteps came back to them a half-beat late, out of sync with themselves, so that every few steps each of them glanced down as if they'd stepped on something.

The academic block was a brutalist's idea of a cathedral. The ceiling rose three floors to a ribbed vault of raw concrete, and the walls leaned inward as they climbed, and the lights — the endless, sourceless, amber lights — were fixed so high that they lit the volume of the space without illuminating a single surface. Everything you looked at directly was in shadow. Everything in the corner of your eye was bright.

"Scale," Elena said, because narrating was how she kept hold of things. "Intentional. You're supposed to feel small in here. It's a — it's an argument in concrete."

"The argument being?" Sarah asked.

"That you are one of a great many, and the building will outlast you." Elena wrote it down, and underlined it, and did not feel better.

Marcus had moved ahead to a junction where three corridors met under a wall-mounted directory — brass letters on dark wood, each line of it intact, none of it faded. He read it aloud in the low voice he used for hazard signs. "Library. Greenhouse. Residential." He put two fingers on RESIDENTIAL and looked back at them. "Left corridor, second junction, down the gallery. Two hundred yards, maybe."

"Two hundred yards," David said. "In a building the size of a city."

"It's two hundred yards," Marcus said, "and I'm counting doorframes while we walk. That's how they did it here — locals read the corridors by counting doorframes, because in a storm every covered walkway looks like every other one. Old town trick." He set off, and after a moment Elena fell in beside him, her pen cap in her teeth.

They counted. Marcus counted out loud under his breath — eleven, twelve, thirteen — and Elena wrote the numbers in the margin of her map, and the corridor went on being exactly what a corridor should be: doors on both sides, evenly spaced, lecture halls and offices with their nameplates polished and their lights on and their chairs pushed in, waiting for a class that had graduated twenty years ago.

It was Sarah who stopped first.

"Listen," she said.

They listened. The building had been loud the whole time, they realized — a fine, continuous presence at the edge of hearing, fans turning somewhere in the walls, pipes ticking as they warmed, the storm reduced to a pressure you felt in the eardrums rather than heard. And now, focusing on it, you could hear all of it stop. Not wind down. Stop. Like a held breath.

Then it started again, one degree louder.

"Ventilation cycling," David said. "Old building. Timers."

"That wasn't a timer," Sarah said. She stood very still with her head on one side — she had been poised her whole life, Elena thought; it was what the camera people had loved about her, that stillness, the sense of a held pose — and then she shook her head slightly and walked on. "You're right. Timers."

They agreed, without saying they agreed, to walk faster.

The library was on their route the way everything in the school was on your route eventually: a set of double doors, a smell of cold paper, and then a room that made everyone stop talking without deciding to.

It rose four floors, floor to vaulted ceiling, in tiers of oak shelving connected by iron galleries and a rolling ladder. The windows — tall, arched, set into the outer wall — were shuttered from inside with heavy panels, and the lights above the reading tables burned green and low over furniture that had been arranged and rearranged by decades of quiet people. The silence had weight here. It pressed gently on the top of the head.

"Jesus," David said, and even that came out wrong, too big for the room.

Elena moved along the nearest shelf without knowing what she was looking for. Spines, alphabetised. She ran one finger across them — a habit from a thousand libraries — and lifted it. Clean. No dust at all. Fifteen years, and the finger came back white.

She wrote that in the margin. *Shelves: clean. Not dusty — clean. Difference matters.*

"Nobody dusts an empty building," she said aloud, to hear how it sounded.

"Somebody does this one," Sarah said. She was standing at the end of the aisle, perfectly still, her bag on her shoulder. "The tables don't match. Look. The two nearest the window have been moved — recently, and not quite back. The legs sit wrong on the boards."

Elena looked. Sarah was right. Two heavy reading tables stood a half-inch out of line with the others, their chair scars facing the wrong way, as if someone had turned them to face the windows and then thought better of it and pushed them back.

"Maybe the caretakers," Elena said. "If the foundation's been sending a crew —"

"They'd face them out," Marcus said. He had stopped at the circulation desk and was reading the ledger left open beneath a glass dome — the paper open to a date, the columns ruled in pencil, every line in the same hand. "Somebody sat here. And whoever it was, they're keeping the book —" He stopped, and let her come read.

Elena crossed to him. The ledger page was dated eleven days ago. The entries beneath the date were in ink, in a careful, upright hand that she did not recognize: names she did not know, times beside them, a column headed *out* filled in neatly for every one.

Eleven days. No names she knew. No explanation of who had written them or why a ledger in a closed school was still being kept by hand.

She copied four of the lines into her notebook — names, times — because copying was what she did when her pulse was up, and because somewhere in her fifteen years of files there was a box of documents about this place and these names might belong to it. Then she closed the glass dome gently over the page, the way you close a cage.

"We're not staying in here," Marcus said.

Nobody argued. The library was beautiful, and it had just told them something none of them knew how to hold, and the residential wing was two hundred yards further on.

The corridor beyond ran along the inside wall of the covered walkway, and through the walkway's glass you could see the storm — a solid white wall sliding past the windows like film through a projector, fast enough to have no detail. Elena walked with her pen cap between her teeth and her map against her forearm, and she was thinking about the ledger, about eleven days, about a hand that wrote neatly in a building nobody used, and she was thinking that she had stood in this exact spot before, aged seventeen or eighteen, and that the memory of standing here was not a memory but a fact in her body: the weight of the backpack on one shoulder, the exact gradient of the slope of that floor. Her feet knew the way to the library. Her feet had known the whole way.

It was the rest that had been blank. Four years, and the corridors came back to her only in pieces — this junction, that stairwell, a door with a scratch on it — with nothing in between to join them. She had asked Marcus once, years ago, why he never talked about school, and he had said *I remember the people, not the place* as if that were an answer, as if anyone remembered people without remembering where they stood.

"Here," Marcus said, and stopped at a door marked VERTICAL GROWING — EAST. "Directory said greenhouse. We cut through, and the residential gallery is on the far side. Saves a circuit."

"Through?" David peered at the door's small window. Behind it, in the dark, something pale moved and then didn't. "Through the... thing?"

"The greenhouse," Marcus said.

"The conservatory. The — yes. Through that."

"That's the route," Marcus said, and opened the door, and warm air moved out past them into the cold corridor, carrying a smell like wet earth and overripe fruit.

The greenhouse was vertical, as promised: a shaft of glass floors stacked one above the other, lit from within by a low, pearlescent glow that turned the falling snow outside into a milky blur. Warm, wet air closed over them like a blanket. The smell was thick enough to taste — loam, chlorophyll, and underneath it a sweetness that was slightly too ripe, the smell of fruit left an extra day on the counter.

Plants grew on every level in disciplined rows: greens, herbs, tomato vines wound up strings to the ceiling, flowers Elena couldn't name going on in defiance of the dark and the latitude and the calendar. Everything here was alive, and nothing here should have been. Outside the glass, Alaska was trying to erase the world. Inside, tomatoes.

"On timers," Sarah said quietly. "The lights, the water — somebody programmed a year of it and walked away."

"The power bill," David said. He had his phone out and was turning slowly, scanning as if the building might have a signal. "Nobody's paying the power bill for this. Fifteen years of — do you know what a grow operation costs to —"

"Somebody is," Marcus said.

He was standing at the base of a planter row, and when Elena came up beside him she saw what he was looking at: the tomato vines had grown out across the maintenance path, thick as her wrist, and someone had cut them back. Not old growth. The cut ends were green and weeping, the blade-marks pale on the stems, and beneath the path the trimmed leaves were still half-laid where they'd fallen — not yet brown, not yet crumbled. Someone had walked this path with shears. Days ago, maybe. Maybe this morning.

Elena took a photograph of the cut stems with her own phone, then wrote down the time in the margin of the notebook, then stood looking at the two records in her hands — the picture and the sentence — and felt the familiar, useless conviction that having evidence was the same as having an explanation.

"We should keep moving," she said.

They crossed the greenhouse single file along the raised path, and the air got warmer and sweeter as they went, and somewhere above them water ticked into a trough on a slow, patient rhythm — *tick... tick... tick* — like a clock in another room. The far door stood ajar.

Elena was the third one through it, and on the other side, in the cooler air of the residential corridor, something inside her chest shifted — a flicker of heat behind the sternum, gone before she could name it, followed a moment later by a dull, possessive ache settling in behind her eyes. She stopped walking. Marcus, two steps ahead, didn't notice; the others had gone on. She stood in the doorway with the wet warmth at her back and the corridor stretching ahead and thought, quite calmly: *That's new.*

She was thirty-eight years old and she had ridden nine hours in the cab of a truck through a white-out on bad coffee and four hours of sleep, and her body was simply sending in its account. It was travel. It was the truck, the cold, the altitude change she'd read about too late. She had a headache. People had headaches.

*Eyes: ache. Chest: warm. Onset: after the greenhouse. Note and continue.* She capped the pen and walked on, and told no one.

The residential wing announced itself the way the library had — by everybody going quiet. It was a long gallery, two floors of it, rooms opening off both sides, each door dark-varnished with a brass room number and a little service hatch for keys, a wall-lamp above every door burning amber on the building's own timer, and at the far end a wide stair going up to a second floor. A fire plan in a glass frame on the wall showed the wing as a grid of small rectangles: *101. 102. 103.*

Marcus went down the gallery trying doors. The first was locked. The second was locked. The third opened on a room made up for someone who was not there — bed tight as a drum, blanket folded to the millimetre, a small desk, a window with the white-out pressing at the glass like something trying to get in. He tried the next: locked. The next: locked. Then, further on, another made-up room, and another after that, and by the time he reached the stair he had the look he got when a number refused to sit still.

"Five," he said. "There are fifty doors in this gallery, and I tried every one. Five of them are open, and all five are made up. The rest have been locked since whenever."

"Rooms with doors," David said, and almost laughed.

"Five rooms," Sarah said. "For five people."

Nobody answered that, because the arithmetic answered it for them, and none of them liked where it came out.

They split to claim them — Marcus and Elena taking 103 together, David and Sarah taking 105 together, because they were married and this was a place that knew their names, and nobody had the good sense to joke about it, Jake taking the last made-up door before the stair and photographing it as he went: the folded linen, the empty desk, the brass number on the inside of the frame. It wasn't trust in the building. It was the opposite — the one familiar thing in an unfamiliar place, a spouse's breathing in the dark, a hand you could find without looking. Two couples, two doors that closed from the inside, and Jake down the gallery with his own. That was the arrangement everybody made without saying it out loud, and it held the way a rope holds when everyone agrees not to test it.

"For the record," he said, catching Elena watching.

"That's my line," Elena said, almost smiling.

Two made-up rooms stood empty — 107, midway, and 109 beyond it, their lamps burning, their beds waiting for nobody. Sarah paused outside 107 with her hand on the doorframe, as if she had half-expected someone to be in it, and then went on to 105 without a word. Nobody switched their lamps off.

She put her bag down on the bed in 103 and sat on the edge of it, and the mattress took her weight without complaint, and the room was warm, and her eyes ached, and from somewhere down the gallery she heard Marcus's steady footsteps doing one more pass of the corridor before he would consent to sleep — because that was what Marcus did: he held the line of a building with his feet until the building proved itself, or didn't.

*The beds were made up.* She wrote it in the notebook, sitting on the edge of the bed, in the flat register she used when she was most frightened. *Fifty doors. Five made up — 103, 105, 107, 109, and Jake's at the end. Couples took 103 and 105. Sheets pressed. Towels folded. Heating on. No dust. Nothing out of place. 107 and 109 still lit, no one in them.*

*The letter went to sixty people.*

*Somebody made up beds for whoever answered.*

She looked at what she'd written. Then, underneath, smaller: *How did they know it would be five?*

It was the question that had been following her since the common room — since the fire, since Jake's toast, since the count that kept coming back the same. Sixty letters had gone out to their class. Five chairs had filled. And here were the beds, turned down and waiting, warm as if someone had walked the wing an hour ago laying a hand on each duvet to check it. Not sixty beds. Five. The building had known how many of them there would be, or somebody walking these corridors had, and either answer was an answer she could not write in a sentence and hand to anyone.

She went to the door to fetch Marcus and see about the crates in the morning, and stopped.

The map. She had drawn the corridors as she walked them — junctions, counts, the directory's three arrows — and the lines on the page were clean and confident, and did not match where she was standing. The library left of the junction, when she had walked out of it on the right. The greenhouse between the academic block and the walkway, when the walkway had been between them. Forty-seven doorframes to the gallery, six minutes at her own watch — a distance you couldn't cross in six minutes, not counting the stop in the library, not counting the greenhouse.

Elena looked at the map for a long time. Then she turned the page and began again from the entrance hall, more carefully this time — and if the first map had been an act of faith, this one was closer to a confession: *this is what I saw; this is what was there; these two things are not the same; verify tomorrow.*

She put the notebook under her pillow — it was the last thing of hers the day had not got at — and lay down on top of the blankets in her clothes, and listened to the building settle around her: pipes, fans, the storm doing its patient work at the walls, and Marcus's footsteps coming back, counting doorframes under his breath, and the small sounds of the others in their borrowed rooms doing the same thing she was. Being careful. In a place that had been kept ready for them.

The wall-lamps in the gallery went out one at a time on their timer, going down toward the stair, and the last light Elena saw before she closed her eyes was the one beside her own door — the only one that stayed lit — as if somebody, somewhere, was leaving one on for her.

And the question sat in the notebook under the pillow, unanswered, in her own handwriting: *How did they know it would be five?*
